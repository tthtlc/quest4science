**Quantum computing uses principles of quantum mechanics—like superposition, entanglement, and interference—to process information via qubits, enabling solutions to complex problems beyond classical computers' capabilities.**[1][2][4]

### Key Principles
Quantum computers differ from classical ones, which use bits (0 or 1) processed through logic gates like AND, OR, and NOT in silicon transistors.[3][7] Instead, they employ **qubits**, the basic unit of quantum information, which can exist in superposition—a mix of 0 and 1 states simultaneously, unlike binary bits.[1][2][4][5][7] For example, two qubits represent four combinations (00, 01, 10, 11) at once, with each added qubit exponentially doubling possibilities (e.g., three qubits: eight combinations).[1]

**Entanglement** links qubits so measuring one instantly affects others, even remotely, allowing correlated computations across the system.[1][2][4][7] **Interference** amplifies correct solutions by having probability waves overlap constructively while canceling incorrect ones.[4]

### How Quantum Computers Work
A quantum computer includes:
- Qubit housing (often near absolute zero or in vacuums to maintain coherence and reduce noise).[2]
- Signal methods (microwaves, lasers, voltage) for entanglement and operations.[1][2]
- A classical computer for programming and control.[2][4]

Computations involve:
1. Preparing qubits in superposition.
2. Entangling them via quantum gates.
3. Applying algorithms (e.g., Shor's for factoring) to create interference patterns.
4. Measuring to collapse states into classical output, yielding probabilistic results.[1][3][4][6]

Quantum processors (QPUs) house physical qubits (e.g., superconducting loops or trapped ions) with control electronics.[4]

### Classical vs. Quantum Comparison

| Aspect          | Classical Computers                  | Quantum Computers                    |
|-----------------|--------------------------------------|--------------------------------------|
| **Unit**       | Bits (0 or 1)                       | Qubits (superposition of 0 and 1)[1][2][7] |
| **Key Features**| Sequential processing, Boolean logic| Superposition, entanglement, interference[2][4][7] |
| **Strengths**  | Scalable, reliable for general tasks| Exponential speedup for optimization, chemistry, cryptography[3][6][7] |
| **Limitations**| Struggles with exponential complexity| Fragile (decoherence), limited data extraction[1][3] |

### Applications and Status
Quantum computers excel in simulating quantum systems (e.g., molecular behavior, protein folding), optimization, and breaking encryption via algorithms like Shor's.[3][6][7] Current systems (e.g., IBM, others) are noisy intermediate-scale quantum (NISQ) devices; fault-tolerant universal ones remain developmental.[3][4]
