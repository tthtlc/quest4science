
Here are 50 short, “viral-friendly” facts about blood clots and strokes, written for everyday readers, not professionals. Always seek medical advice for personal concerns. [cdc](https://www.cdc.gov/stroke/data-research/facts-stats/index.html)

1. A stroke is basically a “brain attack” — most happen when a blood clot suddenly blocks blood flow in the brain. [who](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/stroke)
2. About 87% of all strokes are ischemic, meaning they are mainly caused by blood clots blocking brain arteries. [my.clevelandclinic](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/diseases/5601-stroke)
3. A blood clot in the brain can kill brain cells in minutes, which is why “every second counts” in stroke care. [who](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/stroke)
4. Blood clots are not always bad: your body makes them on purpose to stop bleeding when you get injured. [hematology](https://www.hematology.org/education/patients/blood-clots)
5. Trouble starts when clots form inside blood vessels for no good reason or don’t dissolve properly, blocking blood flow to vital organs. [medicalnewstoday](https://www.medicalnewstoday.com/articles/can-a-blood-clot-cause-a-stroke)
6. A clot that forms in the heart and then travels to the brain is called a cerebral embolism and can cause a sudden stroke. [medicalnewstoday](https://www.medicalnewstoday.com/articles/can-a-blood-clot-cause-a-stroke)
7. A clot that forms directly inside a brain artery on top of fatty plaque is called a cerebral thrombosis and can also trigger stroke. [hematology](https://www.hematology.org/education/patients/blood-clots)
8. Not all strokes are caused by clots: some happen when a brain blood vessel bursts and bleeds, called a hemorrhagic stroke. [who](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/stroke)
9. A “mini-stroke” or TIA (transient ischemic attack) is often caused by a tiny clot that briefly blocks and then clears a brain artery — it’s a major warning sign. [my.clevelandclinic](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/diseases/5601-stroke)
10. Having a TIA can mean a much higher chance of a full stroke soon, which is why it should be treated like an emergency. [medicalnewstoday](https://www.medicalnewstoday.com/articles/can-a-blood-clot-cause-a-stroke)

11. High blood pressure is the number one risk factor for stroke worldwide and can nearly triple stroke risk if uncontrolled. [womenshealth](https://womenshealth.gov/heart-disease-and-stroke/stroke/stroke-risk-factors/health-conditions)
12. Smoking is linked to about two-fifths of stroke deaths in people under 65, making cigarettes a major clot and stroke trigger. [who](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/stroke)
13. Atrial fibrillation (irregular heartbeat) lets blood pool in the heart and form clots that can shoot up to the brain and cause stroke. [womenshealth](https://womenshealth.gov/heart-disease-and-stroke/stroke/stroke-risk-factors/health-conditions)
14. High LDL (“bad”) cholesterol builds plaque in arteries, which can rupture and create clots that block blood flow to the heart or brain. [hematology](https://www.hematology.org/education/patients/blood-clots)
15. Being very inactive, overweight, or having diabetes all raise your risk of both blood clots and stroke. [pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5321635/)
16. Heavy drinking or binge drinking can spike blood pressure and increase stroke risk, especially in younger adults. [nm](https://www.nm.org/conditions-and-care-areas/neurosciences/comprehensive-stroke-centers/before-stroke/risk-factors)
17. Air pollution is now recognized as a modifiable risk factor that contributes significantly to global stroke burden. [who](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/stroke)
18. Stress and depression are not just “in your head”; they are linked to higher stroke risk in large international studies. [pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5321635/)
19. Kidney problems and high fasting blood sugar are on the list of key stroke risk factors that can often be improved with treatment. [womenshealth](https://womenshealth.gov/heart-disease-and-stroke/stroke/stroke-risk-factors/health-conditions)
20. The WHO says most of the global stroke burden comes from just 10 modifiable risk factors like blood pressure, diet, activity level, and smoking. [pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5321635/)

21. In the United States, up to 900,000 people each year are affected by venous thromboembolism (VTE) — serious blood clots in the veins. [pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4715842/)
22. Between 60,000 and 100,000 Americans are estimated to die from these venous clots every year. [cdc](https://www.cdc.gov/blood-clots/data-research/facts-stats/)
23. Venous clots are most common in the deep veins of the legs (deep vein thrombosis, DVT) or as clots that travel to the lungs (pulmonary embolism, PE). [pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2873781/)
24. Hospitalization itself is a huge clot risk: venous clot rates are far higher around hospital stays compared with living in the community (roughly 330 vs 8 per 100,000 people). [pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4715842/)
25. Cancer, recent surgery, pregnancy, and the weeks just after delivery all put people at particularly high risk of venous blood clots. [cdc](https://www.cdc.gov/blood-clots/data-research/facts-stats/)
26. Venous thromboembolism is considered a major public health problem because it’s common, costly, and often preventable. [pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2873781/)
27. People who have had one venous clot are at much higher risk of getting another one later in life. [pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4715842/)
28. Over many years, leg clots can lead to chronic swelling, pain, and even leg ulcers (venous stasis syndrome), which can be disabling. [pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2873781/)
29. Long plane or car trips where you sit still for many hours can slightly increase the risk of leg clots, especially if you already have other risk factors. [nm](https://www.nm.org/conditions-and-care-areas/neurosciences/comprehensive-stroke-centers/before-stroke/risk-factors)
30. Some clotting problems run in families (thrombophilias), meaning genes can make you more likely to form dangerous clots. [pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5321635/)

31. Stroke is one of the leading causes of death and disability worldwide, killing millions and disabling many more every year. [cdc](https://www.cdc.gov/stroke/data-research/facts-stats/index.html)
32. Stroke risk increases with age, but strokes can and do happen at any age, including in young adults and even children. [cdc](https://www.cdc.gov/stroke/data-research/facts-stats/index.html)
33. In many countries, stroke is a top cause of long-term disability, affecting speech, movement, memory, and personality. [my.clevelandclinic](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/diseases/5601-stroke)
34. Rapid treatment can dramatically improve outcomes: certain clot-busting drugs only work if given within a few hours of symptom onset. [my.clevelandclinic](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/diseases/5601-stroke)
35. Mechanical thrombectomy — physically pulling a clot out of a brain artery — can be life-changing for some ischemic stroke patients when done quickly. [my.clevelandclinic](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/diseases/24208-ischemic-stroke-clots)
36. Knowing stroke signs (like sudden face droop, arm weakness, speech trouble) and calling emergency services fast can literally save brain tissue and years of quality life. [my.clevelandclinic](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/diseases/24208-ischemic-stroke-clots)
37. People who survive a venous clot have an increased long-term risk of death compared with people who never had one, making prevention and follow-up critical. [pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4715842/)
38. Simple preventive steps in hospitals — early walking, compression devices, and preventive blood thinners — can greatly cut clot risk in high‑risk patients. [cdc](https://www.cdc.gov/blood-clots/data-research/facts-stats/)
39. After a first stroke, controlling blood pressure, cholesterol, and lifestyle factors can significantly reduce the chance of another stroke. [cdc](https://www.cdc.gov/stroke/risk-factors/index.html)
40. Worldwide studies suggest that if we fully controlled those key risk factors, the majority of strokes could, in theory, be prevented. [pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5321635/)

41. High red blood cell counts can make blood thicker and more prone to forming clots. [nm](https://www.nm.org/conditions-and-care-areas/neurosciences/comprehensive-stroke-centers/before-stroke/risk-factors)
42. Plaque-filled arteries in the neck (carotid artery disease) are a common source of clots that travel to the brain and cause stroke. [hematology](https://www.hematology.org/education/patients/blood-clots)
43. Heart failure makes blood flow slower and more likely to clot, which can then lead to stroke if a clot travels to the brain. [womenshealth](https://womenshealth.gov/heart-disease-and-stroke/stroke/stroke-risk-factors/health-conditions)
44. Some illegal drugs, like cocaine, sharply increase stroke risk by causing blood vessel spasms, blood pressure spikes, and clot formation. [who](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/stroke)
45. Oral contraceptives and hormone therapy can slightly increase the risk of venous clots, especially in smokers or people with inherited clotting problems. [cdc](https://www.cdc.gov/blood-clots/data-research/facts-stats/)
46. COVID‑19 infection has been associated with a higher risk of abnormal blood clotting, including clots in the lungs and brain, in some patients (ongoing research). [pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4715842/)
47. After a major leg clot, the risk of developing chronic leg problems over 20 years can reach 25–40% depending on clot location. [pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2873781/)
48. Good sleep, regular exercise, and a diet lower in salt and processed foods can lower blood pressure and indirectly reduce stroke risk. [cdc](https://www.cdc.gov/stroke/risk-factors/index.html)
49. Quitting smoking starts to reduce stroke and clot risk relatively quickly and continues to drop the longer you stay smoke‑free. [cdc](https://www.cdc.gov/stroke/risk-factors/index.html)
50. Even small changes — standing up more, staying hydrated on long trips, controlling blood pressure, and getting check‑ups — can meaningfully lower the chance of dangerous blood clots and strokes over a lifetime. [cdc](https://www.cdc.gov/stroke/risk-factors/index.html)
